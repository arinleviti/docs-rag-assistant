## Overall picture
Paracetamol is one of the most widely used analgesic/antipyretic drugs worldwide, available both by prescription and over-the-counter. Relatively few clinically significant drug-drug interactions have been confirmed at therapeutic doses — most documented interaction risk relates either to **overdose scenarios** (via the toxic metabolite NAPQI) or to a small number of specific, better-characterised interactions described below.

## Warfarin and other coumarin anticoagulants
This is the most extensively studied and most clinically debated paracetamol interaction.

- **Effect:** Regular, prolonged paracetamol use (at doses ≥2 g/day, and especially at the maximum recommended 4 g/day) may potentiate the anticoagulant effect of warfarin, increasing INR (International Normalised Ratio) and bleeding risk. Occasional/single doses have not shown a significant effect — this matches what both paracetamol SmPCs in this knowledge base state.
- **Proposed mechanism:** Not fully established. One hypothesis: the toxic paracetamol metabolite NAPQI interferes with vitamin K–dependent γ-carboxylase and vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) activity, both part of the vitamin K cycle warfarin also targets. Aging-related reduction in CYP2E1 activity may increase the proportion of paracetamol metabolised via CYP1A2/CYP3A4, which can compete with and inhibit CYP2C9-mediated metabolism of the more potent S-warfarin isomer, raising its concentration.
- **Clinical evidence:** A study of hospitalised geriatric patients (mean age 86) found 10% of paracetamol-treated patients were assessed at risk of a drug-drug interaction, with the majority of flagged interactions involving warfarin; several of these patients showed elevated INR (range 3.2–4.6), with cases of anaemia and haematemesis (vomiting blood) reported. A fatal case (an 83-year-old with atrial fibrillation) involving an untreatable intracerebral haemorrhage has been documented in case-report literature.
- **Practical guidance reflected in the literature:** increased bleeding risk is most associated with paracetamol doses above 1300 mg/day sustained over several days, particularly in elderly patients, regular alcohol users, or those with poor nutrition. Closer INR monitoring is prudent in patients on stable warfarin therapy who begin regular, higher-dose paracetamol use.

## Enzyme-inducing antiepileptic drugs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, primidone)
- These drugs induce hepatic microsomal enzymes (notably CYP pathways), which is the same category of interaction flagged in the SmPC's overdose section — enzyme induction increases the rate of NAPQI (toxic metabolite) production from paracetamol, which is the basis for the lower liver-damage threshold (≥5 g rather than ≥10 g) in patients on these drugs, per both SmPCs in this knowledge base.
- Case-report literature specifically documents acetaminophen toxicity with concomitant carbamazepine use even outside frank overdose scenarios, reinforcing that this is a dose- and duration-sensitive interaction rather than a fixed contraindication.
- Rifampicin and St John's Wort operate via the same enzyme-induction mechanism and carry the same risk-factor status in the SmPC overdose section.

## Cholestyramine
- Cholestyramine reduces paracetamol absorption from the GI tract (consistent with both SmPCs). Activated charcoal has a similar absorption-reducing effect, which is why it's used as an overdose management measure only within 1 hour of ingestion — beyond that window, meaningful paracetamol has already been absorbed, so the intervention loses its rationale.

## Zidovudine
- Regular paracetamol use may reduce zidovudine metabolism, increasing the risk of neutropenia. This interaction is explicitly listed in the oral solution SmPC in this knowledge base but is not mentioned in the tablet SmPC — worth flagging to a prescriber managing an HIV-positive patient on zidovudine, regardless of which paracetamol formulation is being considered.

## Metoclopramide and domperidone
- Both increase the speed (not the overall extent) of paracetamol absorption by accelerating gastric emptying — relevant mainly to onset-of-action timing (e.g. for acute pain/migraine management), not to overall interaction risk or dosing limits.

## Alcohol
- Chronic excess alcohol use is a recognised risk factor for paracetamol-related hepatotoxicity, both via enzyme induction (similar mechanism to carbamazepine/phenytoin) and via glutathione depletion, which reduces the liver's capacity to safely detoxify NAPQI. This is reflected as a risk factor in both SmPCs' overdose sections.

## Overall clinical takeaway
Outside of overdose scenarios or the specific interactions above, no other serious adverse drug interactions with therapeutic-dose paracetamol have been confirmed in the reviewed literature. The strongest clinically actionable interactions for a prescriber to keep in mind are: (1) warfarin/coumarins with sustained higher-dose use, (2) enzyme-inducing antiepileptics/antibiotics lowering the safe overdose threshold, and (3) zidovudine in patients on the oral solution formulation specifically.
